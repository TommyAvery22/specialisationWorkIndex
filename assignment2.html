Parallel Prototypes

Mapping

The main questions I was asking myself when planning and designing the synth, was related to the controls and their relationship between the two players. I questioned how each player should interact with one another, whether they should work with or against each other, play simultaneously or subsequently, and the level of control one player should have compared to their counterpart.

I specifically didn’t want one player to have more control of the synth to the point it leaves the other feeling completely “out of it.” Each of the three mapping prototypes explore the relationship of the inputs between the players and their effect on the output (sound).

Prototype 1: Keyboard Split

The first prototype divides the controls the most obvious and straight forward way: splitting them 50/50, straight down the middle of the “controller.” The simple keyboard controller in a QWERTY layout has one player controlling the left side, and the other controlling the right. In other words, the mapping here allows players to play in parallel with one another because they each have an equal amount of control over the outputted sound. Of course, the issue with this is both players are likely to get in the way of one another because the divided controls are directly next to one another. I considered adding a buffer zone to prevent this but decided against this as it would reduce the number inputs and the division of keys would’ve been unequal.

Prototype 2: Mouse & Keyboard

The second mapping prototype explores the separation of controls by mouse and keyboard. Player 1 plays the notes and can change the wave whereas player 2 can adjust the brightness and echo of the notes being played by moving their cursor around a concealed box. These variables change based on the cursor's position on the x/y axis. These inputs allow players to control different parts of the one sound output.

Commit logs

Prototype 3: Conflict

The third mapping prototype showcases how multiple sounds could work with and against one another. Each player has access to their own keyboard half – like prototype 1. However, some input combinations between the two players can conflict and affect the output. Player 1’s sound can make player 2’s wobble and player 2’s sound can make player 1’s ring. This opens up the potential for users to deliberately work against one another just for the fun of it. Though, in order to produce something satisfying, you need some knowledge of what notes work well together.

Information Design

The three information design prototypes explore a completely different idea for a multiplayer synthesiser: an instrument that is played in a sequence of short turns. The idea is that two players take turns to play a melody that combines to make one continuous composition.

Unlike the previous prototypes, players aren’t playing together but instead, building off of one another's creation. Although this idea always requires one player to sit out – even just momentarily – players are able to work better with one another because they’re creating something based on a frame of reference (the previous turn). In other words, players have time to react to what their counterpart is playing.

The information design of the synth is therefore crucial, because players need to easily know when and where they’ll be involved, down to the second. They need always be aware of their turns length, the time until their turn, and the number of turns until the composition's completion because this could influence how they play. I need to find the perfect duration for each turn, so that players feel as if turns are long enough to develop a melody while short enough so that they don’t become disinterested between each of their turns.

I also considered adding a one-second buffer between turns so both players have time to transfer the controls (keyboard) between one another. However, I didn’t end up including this in the following prototypes.

Prototype 1: Countdown Clock

The first prototype displays the turn duration through a countdown clock in conjunction with a metronome. I was somewhat questioning whether a countdown clock would be too distracting or out of the way whilst the user is playing keys and focusing on the on-screen keyboard.

Prototype 2: Colour Transition

The second prototype looks at replacing the countdown and metronome, and instead indicating the same information through a colour transition to signal the end of a turn. However, without the numbers present, this could be inaccessible to those with some forms of colour blindness. It’s also hard to judge the exact second a turn starts/ends.

Prototype 3: Sound Feedback

The final prototype has some more visual feedback to the way the instrument is played. The visual fading of the keys matches aims to allow the other player to continue playing from the previous melody once their turn begins.
