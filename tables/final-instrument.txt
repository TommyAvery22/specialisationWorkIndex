        <td>TommyAvery22</td>
        <td>Tue Oct 27 15:53:28 2026 +1100</td>
        <td>label each player by their colour in the popup</td>
        <td>add this to the assessment 3 page in the specialisation work index. integrate screenshots from the instrument where applicable: This multiplayer synthesiser can be played by two people, sharing the same computer and keyboard. One player has control over the left half, the other controls the right, with each player affecting a variable that affects the musical output. The yellow player controls the sound&#x27;s distortion whereas the blue player controls its wobble. In addition to this, each player can adjust their sound&#x27;s brightness, wave type, and echo during play. The conflict comes from the other players&#x27; sound and how it affects the combined output. Sometimes the combined variables and keys being played produce a sound that’s pleasing, other times they don’t. Each player has to notice patterns with their partners inputs so they can adjust theirs accordingly. The skill progression comes from trial-and-error. That’s one of its flaws, it’s not an easy instrument to pick-up. You need to have an understanding of what notes work together, be able to read the other players actions whilst playing, know what tempo that should be played at any given moment, and when a variable should be adjusted. Another interactive feature comes from the sound feedback and how that influences how each player plays. Making the distortion and wobble variables distinct enough allowed each player an understanding in how they influence the combined output. Neither player can cooperate if they don’t understand their control. Some of the feedback received in the user testing pointed to feelings of frustration or boredom when the other player had more control over them. The level of control between the two players is essentially equal so that each player feels as if they need the other as much as they are needed by them. With this instrument, each key press also changes the other player&#x27;s sound in real time. When the blue player holds a note, yellow’s sound starts to wobble. Different blue notes give a slower or faster wobble. When the yellow player holds a note, blue’s sound becomes more distorted. Higher yellow notes gives more distortion. When the player lets go, their partner’s sound returns to normal. So neither player controls their own wobble or distortion. They only hear it happening, so they have to listen to each other and respond. The instrument itself is built with[ Tone.js](https://tonejs.github.io/), which sits on top of the browser’s built-in Web Audio system. Each player’s sound travels through a chain of effects: A synthesiser creates the note. Blue’s notes switch on a wobble effect (an auto filter) in yellow’s chain. Yellow’s notes switch on a distortion effect (a waveshaper) in blue’s chain. Each player also has their own brightness, echo and reverb effects. The key presses switch these effects on and off while each person plays. The design system made it as easy as possible for first time players to understand the controls. The opening popup box established knowledge for how the instrument should be played. If this isn’t communicated clearly, then players would easily become frustrated or lose interest. Keeping all of the controls within a confined area on the keyboard helped simplify what is quite a complex instrument to play well. The control variable keys (on the bottom row) are separated from the notes to avoid confusion as to what does what. The layout matches the system with the real world and each player&#x27;s respective controls are a distinct colour. This quickly communicates who controls what and where the controls are, to minimise players having to look down at the keyboard. It was also important to clearly visualise the keys being pressed and the effect that has on the variables, allowing the opportunity for each player to react accordingly to the other players inputs. A fade trail was added so that each player could easily see the key the other player had just played. The length of the fade also matches the current echo setting for that player, so that heard notes can be identified by their played key. There’s also an influence meter above each control so it can be understood how the other players&#x27; inputs affect the sound. These are visually presented so they can be interpreted in each player&#x27;s peripheral vision, so they don’t have to take their eye off what’s most important. Also, each of the variable control keys contain a symbol so users can identify their purpose more easily, without having to read any text.</td>
